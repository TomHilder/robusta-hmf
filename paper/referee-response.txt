> 1. "The major missing component in this paper is the demonstration of the
> eigenspectra, the explained variances, and the distributions of coefficients
> from the RHMF technique. ... The authors can compare the eigenspectra from
> RHMF and from classical PCA and demonstrate how the RHMF can capture the
> underlying data patterns without the effect of outliers."

We agree, and we have added three new figures to the toy-example section
(Section 3.1), all computed under identical conditions for every method (fit
on the same training split; results expressed in a common frame):

(a) Eigenspectra comparison (new Figure 5): the first five basis vectors of 
PCA, RPCA, EMPCA, and RHMF side-by-side with the ground-truth generative basis. 
Because any matrix factorization is identified only up to an
invertible K x K transformation, we rotate the RHMF basis to the principal
axes of its coefficients (the same canonical frame the SVD provides for PCA
and RPCA automatically), and order the true basis by its data-power contribution,
and align component signs. In this common frame the RHMF components more closely
reflect the true components than other methods -- including the absorption-line 
template, which RPCA fails to isolate -- while PCA is noise-dominated beyond 
its first component.

(b) Captured power per component (new Figure 6):
the data power captured by each component as a percentage of the total
mean-square data power, with a fourth panel showing the true per-component
contribution E[a^2]*||g||^2. PCA inflates its trailing components well above
the true values because it absorbs noise and outlier power.

(c) Coefficient distributions (new Figure 7): see M3
below.

Eigenspectra as a function of (K, Q) are now included in two new appendix figures.
Figures 14 and 15 show
the recovered RHMF basis across the full hyperparameter grid. 
The basis is essentially invariant
from Q=2 to Q=10 at the true rank, degrading only for very aggressive

> 3. "Can one also use the coefficient values from RHMF to identify outliers?
> What are the main differences for identifying outliers using robust weights
> and coefficients? Do they complement each other or robust weights are a
> better indicator?"

We have addressed this in two ways.

First, empirically: the new coefficient-distribution figure (Figure 7) 
shows per-component coefficient histograms for PCA, RPCA, EMPCA, and RHMF, 
split into normal and true-outlier spectra. The result is
direct: for PCA, a handful of corrupted spectra acquire extreme projection
coefficients (stretching the axes by an order of magnitude) -- coefficients
are dominated by outliers rather than diagnostic of them. For RPCA, EMPCA, and RHMF
the coefficient distributions are well-behaved, but the outlier spectra sit
within the bulk of the normal population in every component: coefficient
values alone do not separate these outliers. The robust weights (Figure 9) 
remain the primary reliable diagnostic.

Second, we have added three paragraphs to the discussion that explains when each
mechanism is the primary informant: robust weights flag deviations from the
current low-rank model, while higher-K coefficients can reveal shared 
anomalous structure when many outliers deviate coherently. We recommend 
inspecting both, and we connect this to the choice of K and the multimodality 
discussion in Section 2.

> 3. "It is unclear what kind of spectra would be considered as outliers. For
> example, if there are 4 types of stellar spectra ... would the model consider
> the type of stars with 10 spectra outliers? ... This would need to be
> discussed more in the discussion section."

Section 2.1 now defines outliers explicitly as deviations from the
assumed low-rank structure, not membership of a rare class, and works through
the referee's example: four stellar types with counts (100, 100, 50, 10). The
rare type is not flagged as outliers provided its 10 spectra share coherent
low-rank structure; conversely a single deviant spectrum in a populous class
is downweighted. We also explain how the behaviour depends on the choice of
K for multimodal data, and give practical guidance (inspect basis vectors and
shared residual structure). The Discussion returns to this point when
discussing hyperparameter choice and in the new subsection on outlier
identification (see M3).

> 4. "When using PCA, the mean spectrum needs to be subtracted from the
> dataset. Is the same operation needed for this RHMF method?"

Section 2 now states explicitly that RHMF requires no mean subtraction:
the first basis vector is free to capture the mean spectrum (and does so in
practice; see the K1 row of Figure 5), whereas classical
PCA conventionally removes the mean first. We note this in the initialization
discussion, since the SVD initialization is likewise performed on the raw
(not mean-subtracted) data matrix.


> 5. "There are PCA techniques that include weights and missing data, such as
> empca. It is fairer to compare the results from the techniques that also
> consider uncertainty and missing data."

EMPCA (Bailey 2012) is now included in all comparison figures, fit on the same training split as
the other methods and -- unlike PCA and RPCA -- given the same
inverse-variance weights that RHMF uses. 

EMPCA recovers the smooth structure better than PCA (the weights help) but
its basis vectors are visibly contaminated at outlier locations and it fails
to isolate the absorption-line component, because inverse-variance weights
encode known measurement noise, not unknown outliers. Robustness is the
ingredient EMPCA lacks and RHMF adds, and the comparison now shows this
directly (Figures 5--7).

> 7. "Figure 4: the best models with lower KL values have K>=5 and Q=5, while
> the best models for the highest F1 scores have K>=5 and Q=4 and Q=3 ... What
> causes the differences of the best combinations of K and Q values?"

Section 3.1 now explains the discrepancy: the KL-based validation score
and the F1 scores optimize different objectives (calibrated generalization to
held-out data versus detection accuracy at a fixed threshold), so their
optima need not coincide; slightly more aggressive downweighting (lower Q)
trades a small amount of calibration for better outlier separation. We note
that the landscape is flat for K >= 5 and Q >~ 3, that the KL-optimal
hyperparameters yield outlier identification close to the F1 optima, and we
recommend the KL-based score for model selection since it requires no labels.
